The window to win the season opens in February

Spring starts in February for a landscaper's website.

Homeowners start planning outdoor projects in January and February — browsing ideas, getting quotes, deciding who to call before the ground thaws. By April, most have already committed to someone. Your site needs to be visible during those quiet winter months, not scrambling to catch up when everyone's phone is already ringing.

This is the opposite of how emergency trades work. For a roofer, being slow in April still means calls in August. For a landscaper, being invisible in March means half your season is gone before it started. The search window is narrow. If you're not ranking by the time the first warm weekend hits, you're filling your calendar with whoever's left.

A fast, indexed site with the right pages — lawn care, landscape design, hardscaping, seasonal cleanup — shows up in those winter searches. Most landscaping competitors run slow WordPress installs with one "services" page. The bar to outrank them on specific searches is lower than you'd expect.

The transformation photo is doing the selling

A homeowner hiring you can't picture it until they see it. Your photos are the pitch.

Roofing is about trust after damage. Electrical is about safety. Landscaping is different — it's aspirational. A homeowner scrolling through your site is imagining what their own yard could look like. They're not evaluating qualifications the same way they'd vet a licensed electrician. They're deciding whether you have taste, whether your work looks like something they'd want, whether the transformation you delivered for someone else could happen for them.

Most landscaping sites fail this completely. The gallery tab has 40 blurry iPhone photos of finished lawns with no before context. Neither tells a story. The homeowner scrolls, doesn't see themselves in the work, and moves on.

We build the site around your actual transformations. Before-and-after sets for your biggest projects, organized by work type — lawn renovation, hardscaping installs, seasonal planting, commercial property work. Fast-loading, properly sized, displayed where they do the work rather than buried in a gallery nobody opens.

One-time project or recurring contract — your site needs to speak to both

A homeowner who books a cleanup once and converts to monthly maintenance is worth 10x a single job.

Recurring maintenance contracts are the backbone of a sustainable lawn care business. Monthly mowing, fertilization schedules, seasonal planting — these clients pay predictably, refer neighbors, and don't disappear after one job. But they search differently than someone who needs a one-time cleanup. They're not searching "lawn cleanup near me." They're searching "weekly lawn care [city]" or "lawn maintenance company." A site not built to capture both types of searches is leaving one of them on the table every month.

We build landscaping sites with that split in mind: a clear path for project-based inquiries and a clear path for service contract inquiries. Not two sites. One site, built so both types of visitors know immediately that you do what they're looking for.

HOA boards and property managers vet landscapers differently than homeowners do

A property manager reviewing bids isn't looking for inspiration. They want to know you can handle the scope.

Residential homeowners decide based on photos and feel. A property manager or HOA board member deciding on a seasonal maintenance contract for a 40-unit complex wants different things: your commercial references, proof of liability insurance, whether you've handled properties at comparable scale, and what a service agreement actually covers. Your residential portfolio doesn't answer those questions. A site with no commercial section looks like you've never done it.

We build landscaping sites with both audiences in mind when commercial is part of your business. A dedicated commercial services section — organized differently than your residential pages, with the information a property manager actually needs visible without digging — is the difference between making the shortlist and not being considered.

Common Questions from Landscapers

Do I need separate pages for lawn care and landscape design, or does one page cover both?

They're different searches with different intent. "Lawn care [city]" attracts someone who wants ongoing maintenance. "Landscape design [city]" attracts someone planning a project. One page trying to rank for both usually ranks for neither. We build them as separate, indexed pages with separate metadata. See our web design services page for more on how we structure sites.

How do I get booked for the season before spring even starts?

You need to be ranked before people start searching, which means your site needs to be indexed and building authority during winter. A brand new site in March is too late for that season's peak. A well-built site submitted to Google Search Console in October has 4–5 months of indexing time before the planning window opens in February. That's the main reason to launch in fall or winter rather than waiting.

Can the site help me get commercial property maintenance contracts?

Yes, if we build it that way. That means a dedicated commercial services section with the information property managers actually look for: service scope, commercial references, and a contact path that doesn't look like a residential inquiry form. We scope that on the initial call — it's not automatically included if you only do residential, but it's a straightforward addition if commercial is part of your business.

What if I do snow removal in the off-season — can the site handle both?

Yes. That means a dedicated snow removal page with its own metadata. "Snow removal [city]" is a separate keyword with its own search volume and its own searchers. Seasonal service pages indexed separately outperform a single services page listing everything in a paragraph. If snow removal is a service you want more inquiries for, it gets its own page. See our SEO services page for more on how individual pages perform differently.
